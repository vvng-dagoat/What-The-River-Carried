Fictional story

Introduction

Muara Kecil is where the river stops being a river. Upstream it has a name, a market and a bridge. Here it is brown water leaning into the sea, and my town leans with it: stilt houses, drying racks, boats pulled up like sleeping buffalo.

I’m Hana, sixteen. I can read a tide table faster than most people, and I spend school holidays on Pak Long’s boat. He mends nets for a living. He says the sea is a bank: take what you need and trust it to refill.

Past the last stilt-house there is a sandbar where, on the incoming tide, a young green turtle surfaces. One scute is chipped like a bitten biscuit. I’ve never told anyone I look for it.

Kak Dewi runs the river-monitoring post by the jetty. Every Friday she empties the catch-net upstream at the market bridge and writes down what’s in it. “Rivers keep records,” she says. “Most people never read them.”

Fictional story

The Morning After the Flood

The monsoon came two weeks early. For three nights the river climbed the jetty steps, and by Friday it carried the upstream town with it: crates, sandals, a plastic bath. Mostly it carried plastic wrappers, bottles, bags and white foam knotted into rafts as wide as the channel. When the tide turned, the whole mass slid into the mangroves and stayed.

Pak Long’s nets came up heavy and empty of fish. Three days later the turtle was at the sandbar with a loop of green rope around one flipper, dragging a torn bag. Pak Long cut it free. It did not thank us. It went under.

That night, at the meeting hall, the argument began. “This is the city’s rubbish,” Pak Long said. A shopkeeper from upstream answered, “Half of what’s out there is fishing gear.” Both were guessing. Nobody had counted.

“Then let’s count,” I said. I hadn’t known I was going to.

Story note: the turtle’s encounter with the rope is fictional. The research on page 4 describes global debris-ingestion risk, not this turtle or this incident.

9–14 million tonnes a yearPlastic entering the world’s rivers, lakes and seas in 2016 (modelled). If nothing changes, it could reach 23–37 million tonnes a year by 2040.
Estimates vary by scope
Source details

Estimates differ in scope and date, so they are not interchangeable. UNEP’s table lists 9–14 million tonnes (2016, aquatic ecosystems), 19–23 million tonnes (2016, aquatic ecosystems, a different method) and 0.8–2.7 million tonnes (2015, rivers to ocean only; see card 3). The often-quoted 11 million tonnes a year is Pew and SYSTEMIQ’s ocean-entry estimate for 2016, rising to 29 million by 2040; it sits inside the 9–14 range. Limits: model estimates on a 2015–2016 base, not a current-year total. Say “enter rivers, lakes and seas”, not “enter the ocean”.

United Nations Environment Programme. (2021). From pollution to solution: A global assessment of marine litter and plastic pollution (Synthesis). UNEP. ISBN 978-92-807-3881-0. Table 1. malaysia.un.org/…/POLSOLSum_1.pdf

At least 85%of marine litter is plastic.
Published research
Source details

UNEP’s synthesis gives plastic as at least 85 per cent of total marine waste. Limits: this is a share of marine litter, not of everything in the ocean, and the synthesis does not explain the counting method. Citation and link: same UNEP (2021) report as card 1.

More than 1,000 riversIn one global model, they account for about 80% of the plastic that rivers carry to the sea. Small urban rivers are among the most polluting.
Modelled
Source details

The model gives 0.8–2.7 million tonnes a year in total. Limits: it extrapolates beyond where measurements exist, so rankings are more reliable than exact tonnages. It covers rivers only, not fishing gear or direct dumping, so do not add it to card 1.

Meijer, L. J. J., van Emmerik, T., van der Ent, R., Schmidt, C., & Lebreton, L. (2021). More than 1000 rivers account for 80% of global riverine plastic emissions into the ocean. Science Advances, 7(18), eaaz5803. doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaz5803

Up to 52%A global model estimates that up to 52% of sea turtles may have swallowed debris.
Modelled estimate
Source details

Limits: a modelled estimate, not a count of turtles. “Up to” matters. The study covers marine debris, not only plastic. It does not mean every species is equally affected: ocean-stage turtles face the highest risk and olive ridleys are the most at-risk species. Southeast Asia is among the highest-risk regions.

Schuyler, Q. A., Wilcox, C., Townsend, K. A., Wedemeyer-Strombel, K. R., Balazs, G., van Sebille, E., & Hardesty, B. D. (2016). Risk analysis reveals global hotspots for marine debris ingestion by sea turtles. Global Change Biology, 22(2), 567–576. doi.org/10.1111/gcb.13078

About 80% less, not zeroOne major modelling report found that eight measures together could cut plastic leaking into the ocean by about 80% by 2040. More than 5 million tonnes a year would still leak.
Modelled scenario
Source details

The 80% and the 5-million-tonne remainder come from the Pew and SYSTEMIQ report’s System Change Scenario for ocean plastic. The same report found current commitments would cut the flow by only about 7%. Separately, the related Lau et al. paper in Science found that all feasible interventions cut plastic pollution entering aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems by 78% against business as usual in 2040 (and 40% from 2016 rates), with 710 million tonnes still entering ecosystems cumulatively. That is a different scope; the figures are not merged. Limits: modelled scenarios that depend on unprecedented action.

The Pew Charitable Trusts & SYSTEMIQ. (2020). Breaking the plastic wave: A comprehensive assessment of pathways towards stopping ocean plastic pollution. Pew press release

Lau, W. W. Y., Shiran, Y., Bailey, R. M., et al. (2020). Evaluating scenarios toward zero plastic pollution. Science, 369(6510), 1455–1461. doi.org/10.1126/science.aba9475

Fictional story

The Count, and What We Did

We counted on three mornings at low tide, along one 100-metre stretch of bank. Kak Dewi gave me a clipboard, six sacks and one rule: touch everything, trust nothing you haven’t counted. We sorted into six piles, and on the wrappers and sachets we checked every label we could read.

Packaging was most of what we counted: 325 of 412 items. Kak Dewi warned me about the labels. “A label tells you where something was sold, not who dropped it.” They weren’t proof, but about two in three readable labels named upstream shops, enough that Pak Long’s suspicion deserved to be taken seriously.

But sixty-one pieces were rope, net and float, and one green rope I knew at once: same colour, same twist, the knot Pak Long always ties. He turned it over for a long time. “Both,” he said. “It’s both.”

Neither guess matched the count. Fishing gear was nowhere near half the pile, and the labels suggested that some packaging had travelled from upstream. But a label couldn’t tell us who had dropped it. Now we had a count to argue about instead of a feeling.

The town’s plan
  • A proposal for a catch-net at the market bridge. The district authority would assess its design, flood flow and upkeep before anything is built.
  • Refill stations and a bottle deposit, offered to upstream shops.
  • A net-return bin and a repair-first rule at the jetty.
  • A repeat count every season, to see what actually changes.

Fictional story

What the River Carries Now

The district authority took three months to assess the catch-net: where it could be anchored, how it would cope with flood water, who would empty it. It went in at the end of the dry season. By its first Wednesday it was full again. Kak Dewi weighed what it had caught and wrote the number beside the last count.

“One week isn’t enough to know,” she said. “Full could mean it’s working. It could mean the river is still carrying this much. We keep measuring.”

At the next seasonal count, Pak Long’s rope pile was smaller. He had built the return bin himself. The wrapper pile was about the same, and only two upstream shops had signed up for refills so far.

On the first calm morning a green turtle surfaced by the sandbar. I can’t swear it was the same one. I’ve decided not to check.

We did not stop the river. We learned to read what it carries, and to keep reading. A bin, a net and a refill counter will not fix the world’s plastic; page 4 shows how large that problem is. What they gave Muara Kecil was a way to argue with evidence, and a reason to keep counting.

Four things you can do
  1. Count before you argue. Join an organised local litter survey, or ask a local group how to start one safely.
  2. Choose refill and reuse over single-use where you can.
  3. Ask local shops and councils about refill, deposit and collection.
  4. If you work on boats, bring damaged gear back to shore and use return schemes where they exist.
Nets or barriers in waterways need local authority approval. The story’s catch-net is a proposal, not a how-to.
